Types of Power Supplies: What the Ratings Actually Mean

Before comparing specific units, it helps to understand the three specs that actually differentiate one PSU from another: cable layout, efficiency certification, and wattage. Get these three right and almost any reputable brand will serve a 2600 or 3600 build well.

Non-Modular

Every cable is permanently attached to the unit — 24-pin motherboard, CPU 8-pin, PCIe, SATA, and Molex all come hardwired in a single bundle. You install every cable whether you need it or not, which means more clutter to route behind the motherboard tray. It's the cheapest design to manufacture, which is why non-modular units like the Corsair CX450 sit at the bottom of the price range. For a budget build in a case with decent cable routing, it's a fine trade-off.

Semi-Modular

The 24-pin and CPU 8-pin cables are hardwired (since virtually every build needs them), while PCIe, SATA, and Molex cables are detachable. This is the middle ground — you skip installing cables you don't need (like extra SATA runs) without paying the premium of full modularity. The EVGA 500 BQ on this list uses this layout.

Fully Modular

Every single cable, including the 24-pin, detaches from the PSU. You install only what your build needs, which makes cable management dramatically easier in smaller cases and gives the cleanest airflow path. Most PSUs from 550W and up in 2026 ship fully modular, and it's worth the small price bump if your case has limited routing space.

80+ Efficiency Tiers (Bronze, Gold, Platinum)

The 80 PLUS certification measures how much of the power drawn from the wall actually reaches your components versus being lost as heat. Bronze units hit roughly 82-85% efficiency at typical loads; Gold units hit 87-90%; Platinum units push past 90%. Higher efficiency means less wasted electricity, less heat inside the case, and often (though not always) better internal components and tighter voltage regulation. For a 2600 or 3600 build, Gold is the sweet spot — the jump from Bronze to Gold is a modest few dollars, while Platinum adds cost with diminishing real-world returns for a mid-range gaming rig.

Wattage Sizing Basics

Wattage should reflect your GPU, not your CPU — the 2600 and 3600 both draw well under 100W under load. A build with an entry GPU (GTX 1650, RX 6400) is comfortable at 450-500W. A build with a mid-range GPU (RTX 3060, RX 6600 XT, RTX 4060) should target 550-650W. Going bigger than you need doesn't hurt performance, but it also doesn't help it — modern PSUs run most efficiently between 40-70% load, so a massively oversized unit for a modest build just wastes money.

Compatibility Guide: Choosing a PSU for the 2600 or 3600

Match Wattage to Your GPU, Not Your CPU

Both the 2600 and 3600 are 65W parts, so the CPU itself barely factors into your wattage decision. Your GPU choice is what determines the tier you shop in. Look up your specific card's rated TDP and recommended PSU wattage (most manufacturers publish this) before choosing between the 450W and 650W options on this list.

Confirm Your Case's PSU Form Factor and Clearance

Virtually all the units above use the standard ATX form factor, but depth varies by 10-20mm between brands. If you're working in a compact mid-tower or small-form-factor case, check the PSU's listed depth (typically 140-160mm) against your case's PSU bay clearance before buying.

Check for PCIe Power Connectors Your GPU Needs

Older or budget GPUs need a single 6+2-pin PCIe connector; mid-range cards often need two. Newer high-end cards may require a 12VHPWR/12V-2x6 connector, which the Seasonic Focus GX-650's ATX 3.0 design supports natively — other units on this list may need an adapter for that specific connector type.

Don't Buy Used or No-Name PSUs

The power supply is the one component where cutting corners can damage everything else in your build if it fails badly. Stick to brands with a track record — Corsair, Seasonic, EVGA, be quiet!, and Cooler Master all appear on independent PSU tier lists for good reason, while unbranded units sold purely on wattage claims are a common cause of dead builds.

Prioritize 80+ Gold Over Platinum for This Tier

Platinum and Titanium units exist mainly to shave a few extra watts off the wall for high-power workstation or server builds. For a 2600 or 3600 gaming build, Gold efficiency captures nearly all the real-world benefit at a fraction of the price premium.

Leave Headroom If You Plan to Upgrade Your GPU

If there's any chance you'll drop in a more powerful GPU within the PSU's lifespan, buy one tier above your current needs. Going from 500W to 650W today costs relatively little; replacing an undersized PSU in two years costs the full price of a new unit.

How to Calculate Your Wattage Needs

Sizing a PSU correctly isn't guesswork — it comes down to adding up your components' actual draw and applying a sensible safety margin.

Step 1: Start With Your CPU's TDP

The Ryzen 5 2600 and 3600 are both rated at 65W TDP. Real-world draw under full load typically runs close to that figure, occasionally a bit higher under sustained multi-core workloads, but neither chip is a major factor in your total wattage math.

Step 2: Add Your GPU's Rated TDP

This is the number that matters most. An entry card like a GTX 1650 or RX 6400 draws 75-130W. A mid-range card like an RTX 3060 or RX 6600 XT draws 170-190W. A higher mid-range card like an RTX 4060 Ti draws around 160W, while an RX 6700 XT can draw closer to 230W. Use the manufacturer's rated TDP, not marketing "recommended PSU" figures, which are already padded.

Step 3: Add Storage, RAM, and Fans

An NVMe SSD draws roughly 5-8W, a SATA SSD or HDD 5-10W, each RAM stick 3-5W, and each case fan 2-5W. For a typical build with a couple of drives, two RAM sticks, and 3-4 case fans, budget an additional 30-50W combined.

Step 4: Total It Up and Add 20-30% Headroom

Add the figures above, then multiply by 1.2 to 1.3. For example: a 3600 (65W) with an RTX 3060 (170W) and typical peripherals (~40W) totals roughly 275W. Multiplying by 1.3 puts your target PSU wattage around 360-400W — but in practice, we recommend rounding up further to 550-650W, since GPUs draw brief transient power spikes well above their rated TDP, and headroom protects against those spikes as well as future upgrades.

Tip: Don't just look at total wattage — check that your chosen PSU has the specific connectors your build needs. A 24-pin motherboard connector and one CPU 8-pin (sometimes 4+4-pin) are universal requirements; your GPU's PCIe connector count (one 8-pin, two 8-pin, or a 12VHPWR/12V-2x6 connector on newer cards) is the detail builders miss most often when comparing PSU spec sheets.

Step 5: Confirm Connector Types Match Your Components

Every unit on this list includes a 24-pin ATX motherboard connector and at least one CPU 8-pin (or 4+4-pin) connector, covering both the 2600 and 3600's AM4 motherboard requirements. Where units differ is PCIe connector count for the GPU — budget 450-500W units typically ship with one to two 6+2-pin PCIe connectors, while the 650W Gold units on this list ship with two or more, giving you flexibility for dual-connector mid-range and upper-mid-range cards.

FAQ

What PSU wattage do I need for a Ryzen 5 2600 or 3600 build?

It depends almost entirely on your GPU, since both CPUs only draw 65W. For an entry-level GPU, 450-500W is enough. For a mid-range GPU like an RTX 3060 or RX 6600 XT, we recommend 550-650W to leave comfortable headroom for transient power spikes and any future upgrade.

Is 80+ Bronze good enough, or should I get Gold?

Bronze is perfectly functional and safe — it just wastes a bit more electricity as heat compared to Gold. For most builders, the price difference between Bronze and Gold at the same wattage is small enough that Gold is worth it, but Bronze remains a reasonable choice on a strict budget.

Does the Ryzen 5 2600 or 3600 come with a stock cooler that affects PSU choice?

Both ship with AMD's Wraith Stealth or Wraith Spire cooler, which draws negligible power (a few watts) and has no meaningful impact on your PSU sizing decision. Your GPU choice is what drives the wattage decision, not the CPU cooler.

Can I use a 450W PSU with an RTX 3060 or RX 6600?

It's tighter than we'd recommend. Both cards' manufacturers typically list a 550W minimum system recommendation, and a 450W unit leaves little margin for transient spikes or additional components. We'd suggest stepping up to at least a 550W unit for either card.

Is a fully modular PSU worth the extra cost over non-modular?

If you're working in a compact case or care about clean cable routing and airflow, yes — it makes installation noticeably easier and keeps unused cables out of your case entirely. If you're on a strict budget and have a roomy mid-tower with good cable management space, a non-modular unit like the Corsair CX450 works fine.

How long do PC power supplies typically last?

A quality PSU from a reputable brand typically lasts 7-10+ years under normal gaming use, though this varies with load, ambient temperature, and duty cycle. The length of the manufacturer's warranty (3, 5, or 10 years across the units on this list) is a reasonable proxy for how long the maker expects the unit to perform reliably.

Do I need a different PSU if I upgrade from a 2600 to a 3600, or vice versa?

No. Both chips have identical 65W TDP and use the same AM4 socket power delivery, so any PSU sized correctly for your GPU and other components works for either CPU without changes.

What's the difference between 80+ Gold and 80+ Platinum, and does it matter here?

Platinum units are roughly 2-3 percentage points more efficient than Gold at typical loads, translating to a small reduction in electricity cost and heat output. For a 2600 or 3600 gaming build, that difference is minor enough that Gold offers better value — Platinum makes more sense for high-wattage workstation or server builds running near-constant heavy loads.
