How to Overclock a GPU: The Complete 2026 Guide

Marcus Chen, PC Hardware Editor · Updated August 2026

Overclocking your GPU means pushing its core clock, memory clock, and power delivery past the factory-set defaults so it renders more frames per second than it shipped with — typically a free 5-15% performance gain on a modern NVIDIA or AMD card, with zero additional hardware cost. The process is safer than it sounds: today's GPUs have hardware safeguards (thermal throttling, power limits, automatic clock-speed reversion) that stop most overclocks from causing permanent damage. The real risks are milder than people assume — driver crashes, black screens, or a shortened lifespan if you run excessive voltage for years — not an exploded graphics card.

This guide walks through the entire process end to end: which software to use for your card, how to set a baseline before you touch anything, how to raise the core clock and memory clock methodically, how to manage power limit and voltage, and how to stress-test for stability so you don't end up with a card that crashes mid-game. The steps below apply almost identically whether you're running an NVIDIA GeForce RTX card (RTX 40- and 50-series included) or an AMD Radeon RX card (RX 7000- and 9000-series included) — we call out the differences where they matter. Budget about 45-90 minutes for a careful first pass, most of which is spent running benchmarks and watching for artifacts, not clicking sliders.

One more thing to set expectations correctly: overclocking is not a substitute for a bottlenecked system. If your CPU is maxed out at 100% while your GPU sits at 70-80% usage in a given game, more GPU clock speed won't move your frame rate — you're CPU-limited, not GPU-limited, and no amount of core clock offset will fix that. Check GPU usage in your monitoring overlay before you start; if it's already sitting near 99% during gameplay, you're in the right scenario for a GPU overclock to pay off.

Step-by-Step: How to Overclock a GPU

Step 1: Prep Your System and Record a Baseline Benchmark

Before changing a single setting, install the current stable driver from NVIDIA or AMD's website (not a beta), and update your overclocking software to its latest version — old builds sometimes mismatch newer GPU firmware and report incorrect voltage or power figures. Close background apps that use the GPU, like browsers with hardware acceleration and streaming/recording software.

Next, run a benchmark before you touch anything so you have a number to compare against later. Good free options are 3DMark's Time Spy (NVIDIA and AMD), Unigine Superposition, or Unigine Heaven. Run the benchmark three times at stock settings and note the average score, minimum frame rate, and peak temperature — this is your baseline. Skipping this step is the single most common overclocking mistake: without a baseline, you have no way to tell whether your later changes actually helped, hurt, or did nothing.

While you're at it, open your overclocking software's monitoring graphs (Afterburner's OSD or Adrenalin's Performance Metrics overlay) and note your stock core clock, memory clock, GPU temperature, hotspot/junction temperature, and power draw under load. You'll reference these numbers constantly over the next few steps.

What you'll need before starting:

  • The latest stable (WHQL) driver for your GPU, plus your overclocking software of choice installed and updated
  • A benchmark or two — 3DMark, Unigine Superposition/Heaven, or a demanding game you own — to establish and later re-check performance
  • A monitoring overlay running (Afterburner's OSD, Adrenalin's metrics overlay, or a standalone tool like HWiNFO64) so you can watch clocks, temperatures, and power draw live
  • Roughly an hour of uninterrupted time — rushed overclocking sessions are how people skip the stability testing step and end up with a card that crashes days later under a slightly different workload
  • A sense of your PSU's rated wattage and your system's approximate total draw, so you know how much headroom you have before raising the power limit in Step 4

Step 2: Increase the Core Clock Offset

The core clock offset is the setting with the biggest effect on in-game frame rate. In MSI Afterburner or EVGA Precision X1, this is the "Core Clock" slider (shown in MHz above 0); in AMD Adrenalin, it's under Performance → Tuning → Manual → GPU tab.

Raise the core clock offset in small increments — +10 to 15 MHz for NVIDIA cards, +25 to 50 MHz for AMD cards, which tend to have coarser clock-state granularity. After each increase, click "Apply" and run a short stress test (5-10 minutes of Superposition or the built-in AMD Adrenalin stress test) to check for artifacts: flickering textures, colored triangles or squares on screen, driver-crash popups, or a black screen followed by a display recovery message. Any of those means you've gone one step too far — dial the offset back down by the last increment and that's your stable core clock ceiling for now (you'll revisit it after setting power limit and voltage in Step 4).

Most modern NVIDIA cards land somewhere around +100 to +200 MHz core offset before hitting instability; AMD RDNA cards are often more clock-limited and land around +50 to +150 MHz. Don't chase a specific number — chase the point right before instability, since silicon quality ("the silicon lottery") varies card to card even within the same model.

Step 3: Increase the Memory Clock Offset

With your core clock offset locked in from Step 2, move to the memory clock offset — the same software panel, a separate slider usually labeled "Memory Clock" or "VRAM." Memory overclocking tends to have a wider stable range than core clock and often delivers a real gain in memory-bandwidth-limited titles and resolutions (1440p and 4K especially).

Increase memory offset in steps of +25 to 50 MHz, re-testing after each bump the same way you did for core clock. Memory instability shows up a bit differently — instead of artifacts, you're more likely to see stuttering, a driver-timeout error ("Display driver stopped responding and has recovered"), or a full crash to desktop. GDDR6/GDDR6X memory on NVIDIA cards can often take large offsets (+500 to +1,000+ MHz is not unusual on high-end cards), while GDDR6 on AMD cards is typically more conservative — often +100 to +300 MHz. Back off by one increment the moment you see any instability, the same rule as core clock.

Step 4: Adjust the Power Limit and Voltage

Your core and memory offsets from Steps 2-3 are capped by how much power and voltage the card is allowed to draw. Raising the power limit slider — usually expressed as a percentage of the card's rated TDP, up to a hard cap around 110-120% on most consumer cards — gives your GPU headroom to sustain its boost clock under sustained load instead of throttling down. Move this slider to its maximum first if your case airflow and PSU headroom support it (check your power supply's rated wattage against your system's total draw before doing this), then re-run Step 2's core clock test; you'll often be able to push a few more MHz once power limit is no longer the bottleneck.

Voltage is the more advanced lever. Afterburner and Precision X1 expose a voltage/frequency curve editor: click-and-drag points on the curve to either raise voltage at a given clock speed (pushing for higher sustained boost) or, more commonly recommended for most users, flatten the curve to undervolt — running the same or slightly higher clock speed at a lower voltage, which cuts heat and power draw with little to no performance loss. AMD Adrenalin offers a similar manual voltage offset under the same Tuning tab. If you're new to overclocking, we recommend leaving voltage at its default (or applying a mild undervolt) rather than manually raising it — voltage increases are where most of the long-term wear risk lives, and the performance gain from added voltage is usually a couple of percent at best.

Step 5: Run a Full Stability Test

A 5-10 minute check between slider adjustments catches obvious instability, but it won't catch everything — some overclocks pass a short test and then crash 40 minutes into a gaming session once the card has fully warmed up. Once you've settled on core clock, memory clock, and power limit values you like, run a dedicated stress test for at least 20-30 minutes continuously. Good options:

  • 3DMark Time Spy Stress Test — runs the same test loop repeatedly and reports a consistency percentage; anything below 97% suggests thermal throttling or instability
  • FurMark 2 — an intentionally extreme power-virus-style load; useful for finding your absolute worst-case temperature and power draw, though it's harsher than any real game
  • OCCT (GPU:3D or VRAM test) — includes automatic error detection that flags the exact moment memory or core instability occurs
  • Unigine Superposition/Heaven looped for 30+ minutes — a more "real-world" load than FurMark

If the stress test completes without a crash, artifact, or driver-timeout error, your overclock is provisionally stable. We still recommend a final real-world check: play the most demanding game you own for a full session, since some games stress the GPU in ways synthetic benchmarks don't.

Step 6: Monitor Temperatures and Fine-Tune

Throughout every step above, keep an eye on temperature — it's both a safety limit and often the real ceiling on how far you can push an overclock. Watch three numbers in your OSD:

  • GPU core (edge) temperature — keep it under roughly 80-83°C for NVIDIA cards under sustained load; most cards will throttle on their own well before any damage occurs
  • Hotspot/junction temperature — the hottest point on the die, typically 15-25°C above core temp; NVIDIA cards generally stay healthy under ~95°C, AMD cards are rated for junction temps up to ~110°C depending on model, but lower is always better for long-term wear
  • Memory temperature — especially relevant for GDDR6X cards, which run hotter than standard GDDR6; keep it under 100-105°C where your software reports it

If temperatures run hot, your options are: increase fan curve aggressiveness (both Afterburner and Adrenalin let you set a custom fan curve tied to temperature), improve case airflow, or simply back off your power limit and voltage rather than chasing the last few MHz. A cooler, slightly lower overclock that holds its boost clock steady will usually outperform a hotter one that throttles under sustained load — check your final result against the Step 1 baseline benchmark to confirm you actually gained frame rate, not just a higher number on a slider.

Safety and Warranty Warning

Read this before you start adjusting sliders.

  • Warranty: Overclocking through software like Afterburner, Adrenalin, or the NVIDIA App does not void your GPU's manufacturer warranty in most regions (including the US) — but physically modifying the card, removing the cooler, or damage caused by overclocking (e.g., a burned-out VRM from excessive voltage) generally is not covered. Check your specific manufacturer's warranty terms.
  • Change one thing at a time. Adjusting core clock, memory clock, and power limit simultaneously makes it impossible to know which change caused an artifact or crash when something goes wrong. Follow the step order above.
  • Respect thermal limits. Sustained operation above safe temperature thresholds (see Step 6) accelerates long-term wear on the die and VRMs, even if the card doesn't crash immediately. When in doubt, prioritize temperature over clock speed.
  • Increase power supply headroom, don't max it out. An overclocked GPU can draw meaningfully more power than stock. Make sure your PSU has at least 20% headroom above your system's peak overclocked draw — running a PSU at its rated max continuously shortens its lifespan and risks instability under load spikes.
  • Save a "safe" profile. Both Afterburner and Adrenalin let you save named profiles. Keep one at stock settings so you can revert instantly if a driver update or game patch introduces new instability with your overclock.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is overclocking a GPU safe?

Yes, for the vast majority of users. Modern GPUs have multiple built-in protections — thermal throttling, power-limit enforcement, and automatic clock reversion — that intervene before an overclock can cause hardware damage. The realistic risks are software-level: crashes, driver timeouts, or graphical artifacts, all of which are fixed by lowering your clock offsets back down. Damage is rare and almost always tied to running excessive voltage for extended periods, not simply raising clock speeds within the ranges this guide recommends.

How much performance can I gain from overclocking a GPU?

Typical, sustainable overclocks on current NVIDIA and AMD cards deliver a 5-15% frame rate improvement, depending on the card, game, and how much thermal/power headroom you have. Memory-bandwidth-limited titles and higher resolutions (1440p, 4K) tend to show larger gains from memory overclocking specifically. Don't expect a generational leap — an overclock narrows the gap to the next card tier, it doesn't erase it.

Do I need to overclock both the core clock and memory clock?

No, they're independent settings and you can adjust just one. Memory overclocking is generally the lower-risk, higher-reward starting point — it has a wide stable margin on most cards and a noticeable impact at higher resolutions. If you only have time to tune one setting, start there before moving to core clock.

What's the difference between overclocking and undervolting?

Overclocking raises clock speed (and often accepts higher power draw and heat as a tradeoff); undervolting lowers the voltage required to hit a given clock speed, which reduces heat and power draw with little to no performance loss. Many enthusiasts do both together — a mild core/memory overclock combined with an undervolt via the voltage-frequency curve editor — to get a "free" performance boost with lower temperatures and quieter fans than stock.

Why does my overclock crash in games but pass a benchmark?

Benchmarks loop a fixed, predictable workload; real games vary their GPU load second to second and can hit power or thermal spikes a synthetic test doesn't reproduce. This is exactly why Step 5 recommends a 20-30 minute stress test followed by an actual gaming session — if a specific game crashes but your stress test passed, back your core clock offset down by one more increment (10-15 MHz for NVIDIA, 25-50 MHz for AMD) and re-test in that game specifically.

Does overclocking shorten my GPU's lifespan?

A conservative, properly stress-tested overclock run at safe temperatures has a negligible effect on lifespan for most users — GPUs are typically replaced or upgraded well before wear-related failure becomes a factor. The lifespan risk scales with sustained voltage and temperature, not clock speed alone, which is why this guide emphasizes leaving voltage near stock and respecting the temperature thresholds in Step 6 over chasing the highest possible clock number.

Can I overclock a laptop GPU?

Some laptop GPUs support limited overclocking through MSI Afterburner or the manufacturer's own utility, but headroom is much smaller than on desktop cards because laptops have tighter thermal and power constraints. Expect single-digit percentage gains at best, and prioritize temperature monitoring even more closely, since laptop cooling has far less margin before throttling or shutdown kicks in.

What should I do if my screen goes black after applying an overclock?

Don't panic — this is a driver safety mechanism, not damage. Wait 10-15 seconds; the driver usually recovers on its own and resets to safe clocks. If it doesn't recover, force a restart — your overclocking software will not reapply an unstable custom profile automatically on the next boot in most configurations, or you can manually reset to defaults from the software once you're back in Windows. This is exactly why Step 1's advice to save a stock "safe" profile matters.

Bottom Line

Overclocking a GPU in 2026 is a low-risk, genuinely worthwhile way to extract 5-15% more performance from hardware you already own, as long as you follow the incremental, one-change- at-a-time process outlined above: baseline benchmark first, then core clock, then memory clock, then power limit and voltage, then a full stability test, all while watching temperatures the whole way through. Whether you're on MSI Afterburner, the NVIDIA App, AMD Adrenalin, or Precision X1, the underlying method is identical — small increments, frequent testing, and a willingness to back off the moment you see an artifact or crash. Do it right and you'll end up with a stable, faster card and a saved "safe" profile to fall back on if anything ever changes.
